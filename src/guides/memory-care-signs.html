Signs It May Be Time for Memory Care

Caring for a parent with memory loss is hard, and it is often difficult to know when home is no longer enough. These signs can help you decide, and the questions below can help you find a safe place.

Last updated November 2026

1. Start with a doctor's visit

Memory problems are not always caused by dementia. Other health problems, such as depression, diabetes, poor hearing or eyesight, or the side effects of medicines, can also affect memory and behavior. Some of these can be treated, so ask your parent's doctor for a full checkup.

A doctor's report is also needed before your parent can move into a care home in California.

2. Signs that home is no longer safe

  • Getting lost, or wandering away from home
  • Forgetting to eat, or missing or doubling medicines
  • Leaving the stove on or the door unlocked
  • Unpaid bills, or being targeted by scams
  • Falls, weight loss, or not bathing or changing clothes
  • More confusion in the late afternoon or evening
  • New anger, fear or suspicion

3. Signs that you need more help

The person caring for your parent matters too. It may be time for memory care if:

  • You are exhausted, or not sleeping because you watch over your parent at night
  • Your own health, work or family life is suffering
  • You worry about your parent's safety when you are not there

Needing help is not a failure. A good memory care home can make life safer and calmer for everyone.

4. What memory care means in California

In California, memory care is usually given in licensed residential care homes for the elderly: small homes, or special units in larger communities. These homes must follow state rules for people with dementia:

  • Caregivers must have 12 hours of dementia training when they start, and 8 more hours every year
  • Homes may use secured doors or fenced gardens to prevent wandering, but only under state rules, and locks can never replace enough trained staff

5. What to look for on a visit

  • Residents who are calm, clean and taking part in activities
  • Staff who speak gently and guide residents instead of arguing with them
  • A safe, quiet place with a secure garden or walking path
  • Activities that suit people with memory loss, such as music, walks and simple tasks

6. Questions to ask

  • How many caregivers are awake and on duty at night?
  • How much dementia training do your caregivers have?
  • What do you do when a resident tries to leave or becomes upset?
  • Do you use medicines to calm residents? Who decides?
  • Can my parent stay here as their dementia gets worse? Can they receive hospice care here?
  • How will you keep me informed about changes?

7. Free help and support

The Alzheimer's Association runs a free 24-hour Helpline for people living with dementia and their families: 1-800-272-3900.

California's Long-Term Care Ombudsman program helps people who live in care homes, free of charge. If you are worried about your parent's care or safety, call the 24-hour CRISISline at 1-800-231-4024. Every licensed home must post the contact details of its local ombudsman.
